About Me

That uncertainty is what keeps me hooked; I love the challenge of navigating ambiguity and building solutions that matter. Life Story

I'm originally from Pondicherry, a quaint coastal town in Southern India that was a French colony until the 1950s. Growing up, I played street cricket like most Indian boys, but I was also the kid reading books under the shade of catamarans lined along the seashore - surrounded by colonial French architecture and a unique blend of cultures. After college in Chennai, I moved to Chicago to work for Groupon, but when I visited Seattle on a business trip, I fell immediately in love with the mountains and decided to make this city my home.

A quick note on my name: I sometimes go by "Jay" because people find Prasanna difficult to pronounce. While I believe taking the effort to pronounce someone's name correctly shows respect for their journey and origins, I also want to make it easy for people. Jay is actually the shortened form of my father's first initial - in India, we add the first letter of your father's name to your own (like "J. Prasanna Kumar"). By choosing Jay, I'm reminded daily of my humble origins and the struggles my parents overcame to get me where I am today.

I'm married to Nizha, a brilliant and beautiful person who I have the honor of calling my wife. We have three kids: seven-year-old identical twin girls and a one-year-old son. When I'm not working, I'm spending time with them doing all sorts of bucolic things - cooking, gardening, taking on ambitious home projects and failing spectacularly. I love cooking and learning about different cuisines, improvising with whatever ingredients I have on hand. My friends say I should have pursued culinary school after tasting my food - though that might just be because they want to stay friends and keep getting invited to dinner.

Some fun facts:

  • I'm an endurance biking enthusiast and have ridden the Seattle to Portland 200+ mile journey multiple times - once even with a broken wrist and a partially working gear on my bike

How I View Success

Great leaders inspire and create energy by setting a vision and aligning everyone to work towards achieving it. This is what I strive for every day. I don't just want people to be satisfied with their work - I want them to be excited and passionate about it. This happens when someone's skills + values + mission align. Not everyone knows this alignment from the start, but great leaders build teams where everyone discovers this alignment. When such a team comes together, they solve problems everyone else thought were impossible.

Achieving business outcomes is the key purpose for why a team exists. My role as a leader is to drive clarity around this business purpose and build a high-performing team to achieve it as efficiently as possible. I know it's working when the team exhibits high trust, psychological safety, ownership, and consistent execution.

I feel successful when I've brought clarity to a messy situation or when I help my team ship something impactful for the business.

How I Communicate

My philosophy: Communication is the biggest part of my job, so I'm deliberate about it. I default to written communication - docs, posts, and structured artifacts over meetings and 1:1 conversations. My framework: if something needs to be understood by more than 3 people and takes more than 10 minutes to explain individually, write a doc.

In meetings, I'm usually quiet because I'm actively listening and understanding everyone's role in the play. I speak up when asked directly, when the group needs direction, or when I sense the meeting is off track.

How to reach me:

  • Slack/DM: Quick questions, time-sensitive things (30 min response during work hours, 24 hours otherwise)
  • Doc: Anything with trade-offs or needing thoughtful input
  • Post: To keep me informed (I'll read and comment)
  • Urgent (SEV, outage): Visible channels (posts/group chats) + structured tools (SEV docs, investigation docs)

Feedback: Coaching is verbal and immediate. Sensitive feedback is written for precision but delivered face-to-face, with a heads-up so you're ready to listen actively.

I know asking for docs can be frustrating at first, but it scales. When people resist, I help by creating a starter doc with structure to make it easier.

What I Expect from People I Support

Think beyond yourself. Stellar performance means caring about the team and org, not just your own tasks. Help solve problems even when you're not explicitly in charge. Be thoughtful about trade-offs and uncompromising on quality.

Be proactive, not passive. If you're waiting to be told what to do, you're not doing a great job. Seek out information and ask questions proactively. If you're building something that leaves messes for others to clean up, that's a big miss - it shows you don't care about your teammates.

Practice radical candor. I follow radical candor and expect the same from you. Give me hard feedback without beating around the bush. I'm not perfect and I make mistakes, but I love overcoming problems and becoming better. If you're challenging my decision upfront, I'll happily spend an hour discussing it with you - to understand your perspective and change my decision, change your mind, or make sure you're committing with reservations understood.

Communicate in structured ways. I expect you to write docs and use structured artifacts. If you struggle with blank-page syndrome, I'll create scaffolds - team wikis, docs, tasks - for you to contribute to rather than spinning up docs endlessly.

High autonomy with over-alignment. I expect you to work with a very high level of autonomy, but with over-alignment across stakeholders. I like building tightly knit teams where everyone looks out for others and goes out of their way to help.

How I Give and Receive Feedback

My philosophy: I follow radical candor. I give continuous feedback, not just in 1:1s, but I'm focused - usually 1-2 positive items and 1 constructive item, not a laundry list.

I always start with the positive. Before giving critical feedback, I deliberately talk about positive things or thank you for something you did well. People listen to critical feedback better when they trust that I value their work.

How I deliver it:

  • Coaching feedback: Verbal and immediate with follow-up actions
  • Sensitive feedback: Written for precision, delivered face-to-face, with a heads-up so you're ready to listen
  • I ensure follow-up: You'll either know how to act on it, or I'll check in during our next 1:1

I tailor feedback to you: For junior folks, I'm more prescriptive. For senior folks, I give room to come up with your own response while supporting whatever you need. No rigid framework - it's fluid based on person and situation.

Public vs. private: I praise publicly and criticize privately. Challenge or debate me in meetings (without derailing the agenda), but save personal feedback for 1:1s.

How to give ME feedback: Be straight and start with what I'm not doing well, then provide context by explaining the situation and my behavior. The sooner you contact me about something non-optimal, the better - it helps me adjust and make positive impact. DM me about this stuff.

What Drives Me Nuts

A job not well done. Compromising on quality, not thinking through repercussions, and leaving a mess for others to clean up. This shows you don't care about your teammates, and I have zero tolerance for that.

Saying you'll do something and not doing it on time. Delays happen - I'm okay with that. But manage it upfront. Don't let me find out at the deadline that something isn't done.

Lack of ownership. When people pass things along saying "not my problem" and don't show up for their team, it drives me nuts. We're a team - we need to look out for each other.

Things I Do That May Annoy You

I nudge about document hygiene. I might nudge you often to maintain proper document hygiene - resolving comments if they've been addressed, recording sign-offs, publishing in the right groups for visibility. I know it can feel nitpicky, but it's how we scale communication.

I ask for data even when you have strong opinions. It's not because I don't believe in your expertise - I do. But I want to form my own opinion, and data is the best way to be objective about it. I know this can feel like analysis paralysis, but I have strategies to avoid that trap: making deliberate milestones to gather data, building prototypes to validate hypotheses quickly, or looking for proxy metrics that show we're moving in the right direction.
